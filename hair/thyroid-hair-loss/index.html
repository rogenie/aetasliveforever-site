Thyroid and hair loss: which tests to ask about

Thinning hair is one of the listed symptoms of an underactive thyroid, and thyroid problems are common in women in midlife. Here’s what the official sources and research say, and what testing usually looks like.

By the ÆTAS Research Desk · Checked against sources November 21, 2026 · How we work

Quick answer. Yes, thyroid problems can be linked to hair loss in women. NIH lists dry, thinning hair as a symptom of an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism), and a 2024 study of 500 women with sudden shedding found an underactive or overactive thyroid in 252 of them. The usual first test is a TSH blood test, often followed by free T4 and other thyroid tests if the result is off. Symptoms alone can’t diagnose it.

Can thyroid problems cause hair loss?

Your thyroid is a small gland in your neck that makes hormones controlling how your body uses energy. It can make too little (hypothyroidism, or underactive thyroid) or too much (hyperthyroidism, or overactive thyroid).

The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) lists “dry, thinning hair” among the symptoms of hypothyroidism, along with fatigue, weight gain, trouble tolerating cold, joint and muscle pain, dry skin, heavy or irregular periods, a slowed heart rate and depression. The US Office on Women’s Health lists dry, thinning hair too.

NIDDK’s hyperthyroidism page doesn’t list hair changes. Its symptoms include weight loss despite a bigger appetite, a fast or irregular heartbeat, nervousness, trouble sleeping, shaky hands, sweating and frequent bowel movements.

Hypothyroidism develops slowly, so NIDDK says you may not notice symptoms for months or even years.

How common is thyroid disease in women?

  • Underactive thyroid: NIDDK says nearly 5 out of 100 Americans 12 and older have hypothyroidism, most with mild or few obvious symptoms. Women are much more likely than men to develop it, and it’s more common after 60.
  • Overactive thyroid: about 1 in 100 Americans 12 and older, more often women and people over 60.
  • Over a lifetime: the Office on Women’s Health says one in eight women will develop a thyroid problem. It says women are especially likely to develop thyroid disease right after pregnancy and after menopause.

The same office notes that thyroid symptoms are sometimes mistaken for menopause symptoms. That matters in your 40s, when shedding, tiredness and period changes can all have more than one cause.

NIDDK names Hashimoto’s disease, an autoimmune condition, as the most common cause of hypothyroidism, and Graves’ disease as the most common cause of hyperthyroidism.

What one study of thyroid and hair loss found

A 2024 study in the journal Medicine (Bin Dayel and colleagues) looked back at records from one dermatology clinic in Saudi Arabia from 2012 to 2022.

DetailWhat the study reported
Who500 women 18 and older with telogen effluvium (sudden, diffuse shedding) who had thyroid blood tests
DesignRetrospective (a look back at existing records), single clinic
Thyroid results248 normal, 150 underactive, 102 overactive
SeverityWomen with an underactive thyroid had more severe hair loss on average; 34% had severe loss, vs 18% of women with normal thyroid tests and 20% with an overactive thyroid

The authors concluded that thyroid problems, especially hypothyroidism, are a common cause of telogen effluvium, and that thyroid testing belongs in the workup. The limits are real: this was one clinic, looking backward, with women who had already been sent for testing, so it can’t tell you how likely thyroid disease is for any one woman with shedding. The authors also note that hair-loss scoring was subjective.

Researchers think an underactive thyroid disrupts the hair growth cycle, holding more hairs in the resting phase, according to the study’s discussion. How an overactive thyroid affects hair is not fully understood. For more on that resting-phase shedding, see telogen effluvium.

Thyroid tests for hair loss: what to ask about

NIDDK is clear that a hypothyroidism diagnosis can’t be based on symptoms alone. Doctors use a history, an exam, and blood tests. According to MedlinePlus (from the National Library of Medicine) and the Office on Women’s Health:

TestWhat it measures
TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone)A hormone from the pituitary gland that tells the thyroid how much hormone to make. When thyroid hormone is low, TSH goes up; when it’s high, TSH drops. Usually the first test
T4 (free or total)The main thyroid hormone. Often ordered if TSH is abnormal
T3Another thyroid hormone, sometimes checked too
Thyroid antibodiesHelp check for autoimmune causes such as Hashimoto’s or Graves’ disease

MedlinePlus notes that TSH alone can’t show what is causing a thyroid problem, and that pregnancy, serious illness and older age can shift results. Ask before your blood draw whether you need to stop any medicine, but don’t stop one unless you’re told to.

One easy-to-miss detail: high-dose biotin, common in hair supplements, can skew thyroid test results. NIH’s Office of Dietary Supplements describes cases where biotin made results falsely look like an overactive thyroid. Tell your clinician and lab if you take it; see biotin for hair.

The Office on Women’s Health says screening for thyroid disease isn’t recommended for most women. Testing is about your symptoms and history, so describe the shedding, when it started and anything else you’ve noticed.

Will hair grow back after thyroid treatment?

None of the official sources we checked give a timeline for hair recovery after thyroid treatment, and the 2024 study didn’t follow women after treatment. What is clear is that a thyroid problem is a cause to treat directly. The label for over-the-counter minoxidil for women says it won’t help hair loss related to hypothyroidism.

Thyroid treatment is a decision for you and your clinician. Don’t start, stop or change a thyroid medicine based on your hair.

What this means for you

  • Thinning hair plus tiredness, feeling cold, weight or period changes is worth raising with your clinician, especially if thyroid problems run in your family.
  • Thyroid isn’t the only lab cause. MedlinePlus says clinicians check for thyroid disease and iron deficiency when assessing female pattern hair loss; see iron, ferritin and hair loss.
  • Hair loss often has more than one cause in midlife. Start with hair loss in women: causes and how to tell them apart.

Questions to ask your clinician or a board-certified dermatologist:

  • Should my thyroid be checked, and which tests will you order?
  • If my TSH is normal, do I need any other thyroid tests?
  • Could any supplement I take, like biotin, affect the results?
  • What else could be causing my shedding?
