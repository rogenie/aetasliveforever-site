Spironolactone for female pattern hair loss: the evidence

Spironolactone is a decades-old blood pressure and heart medicine that dermatologists sometimes prescribe for thinning hair in women. Here’s what the studies show, what they don’t, and what to ask before trying it.

By the ÆTAS Research Desk · Reviewed by the ÆTAS editorial team, November 22, 2026 · Checked against sources November 21, 2026 · How we work

Quick answer. Spironolactone is not FDA-approved for hair loss, but it’s prescribed off-label for female pattern hair loss as an anti-androgen. A 2023 meta-analysis of 5 studies found that 56.6% of patients improved, more when it was combined with minoxidil. The evidence is mostly small, non-randomized studies, and the drug’s label says to avoid it in pregnancy because of possible effects on a male fetus.
Review  Evidence at a glance
Best evidence
A 2023 meta-analysis (Cureus, 5 studies, 413 patients) and a 2023 systematic review (Clinical, Cosmetic and Investigational Dermatology, 7 studies, 618 patients)
Women included?
Yes. The oral studies in the 2023 systematic review were all in women (295)
Dose or protocol tested
Oral doses from 25 mg to 200 mg a day, mostly 80 to 110 mg, usually for 6 months to over a year
Bottom line
Promising but low-quality evidence, with only one or two randomized trials. A prescription decision that needs a clinician and some monitoring

What is spironolactone, and why is it used for hair loss?

Spironolactone was first approved in the US in 1960. Its FDA-approved uses, per the current label, are certain kinds of heart failure, high blood pressure, fluid buildup from liver or kidney disease, and a hormone condition called primary hyperaldosteronism. Hair loss is not on the list.

Dermatologists use it “off-label” (for a use the FDA hasn’t approved) because it acts as an anti-androgen. Androgens are hormones such as testosterone that can hold back scalp hair follicles, according to a 2022 dermatology review by Kamp and colleagues. MedlinePlus lists it among the options when topical minoxidil, the only FDA-approved medicine for female pattern hair loss, doesn’t work. The Kamp review lists it among treatments for menopause-related hair loss.

Off-label prescribing is legal and common, but it means the drug hasn’t gone through FDA review for this use.

Spironolactone for female pattern hair loss: what the studies show

Two 2023 reviews pulled the research together:

ReviewWhat was includedMain findings
Aleissa, Cureus (systematic review and meta-analysis)5 studies of oral spironolactone for female pattern hair loss, 413 patients; only one was a randomized trial56.6% improved overall; 65.8% when combined with minoxidil vs 43.2% on spironolactone alone. Results varied a lot between studies
Wang and colleagues, Clinical, Cosmetic and Investigational Dermatology (systematic review)7 studies of androgenetic alopecia, 618 patients (553 women); 5 oral studies, all in women; 2 randomized trials81% (158 of 195) reported better hair growth in four oral studies; combination with minoxidil did better than single treatment in the three studies that compared them

A few individual studies give a sense of the details:

  • Sinclair, 2005: 80 women followed for an average of 16 months on oral anti-androgens; 80% had no further progression or improved.
  • Famenini, 2015: 19 women; 74.3% reported improvement or stabilization.
  • Sinclair, 2018: 100 women on low-dose spironolactone plus low-dose oral minoxidil for 12 months; shedding and density scores improved over time. There was no comparison group.
  • Liang, 2022: the one randomized trial in the meta-analysis, 115 patients over 24 weeks. Topical minoxidil plus microneedling did better than minoxidil plus oral spironolactone or minoxidil alone.

Doses in the studies ranged from 25 to 200 mg a day, most between 80 and 110 mg. These are the doses that were tested, not a recommendation; dosing is for your clinician to decide.

Both reviews call for better trials. The meta-analysis author noted the small number of patients, the mix of study designs and doses, and wide variation in results. The systematic review authors couldn’t pool results because studies measured hair differently. The meta-analysis reported no funding; the systematic review was funded by a national science grant in China, and both reported no conflicts of interest.

Side effects of spironolactone

In the hair-loss studies, side effects were usually mild. In the systematic review’s safety data from 216 people on oral spironolactone:

Side effectHow often
Menstrual changes7.9%
Dizziness or headache6.5%
Unwanted facial hair4.1%
High potassium0.9% (2 people)
Stopped because of side effects3.2%

The authors noted side effects were more common at 80 to 110 mg a day than at 25 mg. In one randomized trial in the meta-analysis, 40.5% of patients reported menstrual changes, so rates differ a lot between studies.

The FDA label lists warnings for high potassium, low blood pressure, worsening kidney function and other electrolyte changes, and says potassium should be checked within a week of starting and regularly after. It lists irregular or missed periods, postmenopausal bleeding, and breast and nipple pain among reproductive side effects. MedlinePlus advises avoiding potassium-containing salt substitutes. Drugs that raise potassium, including potassium supplements, some blood pressure medicines (ACE inhibitors and ARBs), NSAID pain relievers and trimethoprim, can interact.

MedlinePlus also notes that spironolactone has caused tumors in laboratory animals. That is an animal finding; ask your prescriber what it means for you.

Why spironolactone isn’t used in pregnancy

The FDA label says that, based on animal studies, spironolactone may affect how a male fetus develops sexually. In rats given high doses late in pregnancy, male fetuses showed feminization. The label says to avoid spironolactone in pregnancy or advise of the potential risk to a male fetus. If you could become pregnant, ask your clinician about this before starting.

What this means for you

  • Spironolactone has some evidence for female pattern hair loss, especially alongside minoxidil, but the studies are small and mostly not randomized.
  • It’s a prescription drug with real monitoring needs, especially for potassium and kidney function.
  • Get the cause confirmed first. Shedding from telogen effluvium, thyroid problems or low iron needs a different approach. See hair loss in women: causes.

Questions to ask a board-certified dermatologist:

  • Is my hair loss female pattern hair loss, and how sure are you?
  • Should I try topical minoxidil first, or combine treatments?
  • Do my other medicines or health conditions make spironolactone risky for me?
  • Which blood tests will I need, and how often?
  • What should I do about contraception while taking it?
  • How long before we judge whether it’s working?
