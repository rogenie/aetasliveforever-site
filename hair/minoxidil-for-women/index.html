Minoxidil for women: topical vs oral, what the trials show

If your part is widening or your ponytail feels thinner, minoxidil is probably the first treatment you’ll hear about. It comes as a scalp foam or solution, and sometimes as a low-dose pill. Here’s what the labels and trials say about each.

By the ÆTAS Research Desk · Reviewed by VITA, our AI editorial reviewer · Checked against sources November 15, 2026 · How we work

Quick answer. Topical minoxidil is the only medicine the FDA has approved for female pattern hair loss. In a 48-week trial of 381 women, both the 5% and 2% solutions beat placebo, and MedlinePlus says it regrows hair in about 1 in 4 or 5 women and slows loss in most. Low-dose oral minoxidil is used off-label for hair loss: small trials in women look promising, but it isn’t FDA-approved for this use and has different side effects.
Human trial  Evidence at a glance
Best evidence
A 48-week randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 381 women (Lucky et al., 2004) for topical minoxidil; small randomized trials summarized in a 2023 review for oral minoxidil
Women included?
Yes. All the hair-loss trials described here studied women with female pattern hair loss; the topical trial enrolled women aged 18–49
Dose or protocol tested
Topical: 5% or 2% solution twice daily for 48 weeks. Oral (off-label): 0.25 mg to 1.25 mg a day for 6 to 24 months in the trials reviewed
Bottom line
Topical minoxidil has FDA approval and placebo-controlled evidence in women. Oral minoxidil is promising but off-label, with smaller and shorter studies. Both only work while you keep using them

What is minoxidil, and how is it approved for women?

Minoxidil is also a prescription blood pressure pill, and extra hair growth is a well-known side effect of the tablets. The two forms have very different official status:

  • Topical minoxidil (foam or solution, sold over the counter). According to MedlinePlus, the National Library of Medicine’s health site, it’s the only medicine the FDA has approved for female pattern hair loss. MedlinePlus notes that the 2% solution or 5% foam is what’s used for women.
  • Oral minoxidil (prescription tablets). The FDA label approves it only for severe high blood pressure that hasn’t responded to a diuretic plus two other blood pressure drugs. Using it for hair loss is off-label, a 2023 review in Skin Appendage Disorders confirms.

Female pattern hair loss is gradual thinning over the top of the head, usually with the front hairline kept. If your loss looks different, start with the main causes of hair loss in women. Minoxidil is labeled for pattern thinning, not for sudden or patchy loss.

Topical minoxidil for women: what the trial found

The largest placebo-controlled trial in women was published in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology in 2004 (Lucky et al.).

  • Who: 381 women aged 18 to 49 with female pattern hair loss, at multiple centers.
  • Design: randomized and double-blind. 153 women used 5% minoxidil solution, 154 used 2%, and 74 used a placebo solution, each twice a day.
  • How long: 48 weeks.
  • Measured: the change in the count of non-fine (nonvellus) hairs, plus the women’s own and the investigators’ ratings of growth.
  • Found: 5% beat placebo on all three measures. 2% beat placebo on hair count and investigator rating, but not on the women’s own rating. Women rated 5% as more helpful than 2%.

Itching, scalp irritation and unwanted hair growth on the face or body were more common with 5% than with 2% or placebo. The authors found no signs of effects elsewhere in the body. The trial was funded by Pfizer (formerly Pharmacia and Upjohn).

MedlinePlus puts the real-world picture plainly: minoxidil may regrow hair in about 1 in 4 or 5 women, and may slow or stop loss in most. It slows thinning more reliably than it restores a full head of hair.

The shedding phase: why hair can fall out at first

Many women quit in the first weeks because they see more hair in the brush. The consumer leaflet on a 5% foam for women, posted on the NIH’s DailyMed label database, warns that shedding may increase for up to 2 weeks after starting. It calls this expected and temporary, and says to see a doctor if it goes on longer.

Results take patience. The same label says regrowth may show at about 3 months, and some women need at least 6 months. MedlinePlus says to allow at least 4 months, and possibly up to a year. The label also advises stopping and asking a doctor if there’s no regrowth after 6 months.

Minoxidil works only while you use it. The label says regrown hair is likely to be lost within 3 to 4 months of stopping.

Who the label says shouldn’t use topical minoxidil

The over-the-counter label for women lists several situations where it shouldn’t be used without a doctor, because the hair loss may have another cause:

  • Hair loss that is sudden, patchy or linked to childbirth, or a cause you don’t know
  • No family history of hair loss
  • A red, inflamed, infected, irritated or painful scalp
  • Under 18, or using other medicines on the scalp
  • Heart disease (ask a doctor first)
  • Pregnancy or breastfeeding (the label says it may be harmful)

It also says to stop and ask a doctor for chest pain, a fast heartbeat, faintness or dizziness, sudden weight gain, swollen hands or feet, scalp irritation, or unwanted facial hair. Infants have grown extra hair after skin contact with a caregiver’s treated scalp; the label says it went away.

Oral minoxidil for women: what’s known so far

Low-dose oral minoxidil, often called LDOM, is prescribed off-label for hair loss. The 2023 review by Gupta and colleagues summarizes the trials in women with pattern hair loss:

StudyDesignWhat was comparedResult
Ramos et al., 2020Randomized, open-label (not blinded), 24 weeks1 mg oral vs 5% topicalTrend favoring oral; difference not statistically significant
Vahabi-Amlashi et al., 2021Randomized pilot, 6 months0.25 mg oral vs 2% topicalNo significant difference
Nascimento e Silva et al., 2022Randomized, double-blind, 24 weeks0.25 mg vs 1 mg oral1 mg more effective

These are the doses the studies tested, not recommendations. None of these trials compared oral minoxidil with a placebo, and all were small and lasted a year or less.

Side effects of oral minoxidil

  • Unwanted hair growth (on the face or body) is the most common. In a 1,404-patient study summarized in the review, 20.1% of women had it, at a median dose of about 1.1 mg a day.
  • Dizziness, fast heartbeat and fluid swelling were less common. In one small study, 3 of 12 women had swelling of the limbs.
  • Pregnancy: the review says oral minoxidil isn’t recommended in pregnancy or breastfeeding, and lists heart rhythm problems, kidney disease and low blood pressure among reasons for caution.

For context, the FDA label for the blood pressure version carries a boxed warning about fluid building up around the heart. At blood pressure doses, about 3% of patients not on dialysis developed it, temporary swelling occurred in 7%, and about 80% had extra body hair growth. Those numbers come from people with severe high blood pressure, not from women on low doses for hair. They’re why oral minoxidil needs a prescriber who knows your heart and blood pressure history.

What this means for you

If a dermatologist confirms female pattern hair loss, topical minoxidil is the treatment with FDA approval and placebo-controlled evidence in women. Expect a possible shed early on, give it months, and know it’s a long-term commitment. Oral minoxidil is a reasonable thing to ask about, but it’s off-label and its evidence is thinner. For other options with lighter evidence, see what the rosemary oil trial found, and for lab causes worth ruling out first, iron, ferritin and hair loss.

Questions to ask a board-certified dermatologist:

  • Is this female pattern hair loss, or could shedding or another cause be part of it?
  • Is topical minoxidil a good fit for me, and how will we judge whether it’s working?
  • Would oral minoxidil make sense for me, given my blood pressure, heart health and other medicines?
  • What side effects should make me call you?
  • What if I’m planning a pregnancy or breastfeeding?

Don’t start, stop or change any prescription without the clinician who prescribes it. More in the Hair hub and our guide to hair thinning in perimenopause.
