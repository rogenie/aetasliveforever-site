Hair thinning in perimenopause: causes and what helps

A wider part, a see-through crown, more hair in the brush. Hair changes are common in the years around menopause. Here’s what the research says is going on and which options have evidence behind them.

By the ÆTAS Research Desk · Reviewed by VITA, our AI editorial reviewer · Checked against sources November 21, 2026 · How we work

Quick answer. Hair thinning in perimenopause is often female pattern hair loss, which a 2022 dermatology review says commonly shows up around menopause, when falling estrogen is thought to play a part. Thyroid disease and low iron are worth ruling out, and topical minoxidil is the only FDA-approved medicine for female pattern hair loss. Evidence for other options is thinner.
Review  Evidence at a glance
Best evidence
A 2022 review on menopause and hair (Clinical and Experimental Dermatology); FDA-approved labeling for topical minoxidil; 2023 reviews of spironolactone
Women included?
Yes. The reviews focus on women; the spironolactone reviews pooled studies of mostly or only women
Dose or protocol tested

Bottom line
Find the cause first. Minoxidil has the strongest evidence; spironolactone, PRP and lasers have some; HRT and most supplements lack good evidence for hair

Why does hair thin in perimenopause?

Perimenopause is the run-up to menopause, when ovulation and periods become irregular and hot flashes may start. Menopause usually happens between 45 and 55, according to a 2022 review by Kamp and colleagues in Clinical and Experimental Dermatology.

Estrogen is thought to keep hairs in their growing phase longer. As estrogen falls, the review says, studies have found a smaller share of growing hairs in postmenopausal women, more over the front and top of the scalp than the back. Androgens (hormones such as testosterone) can hold back scalp follicles, but the review says falling estrogen is thought to be the bigger factor in women. Most women with this kind of thinning have normal androgen levels. Genes matter too.

MedlinePlus adds that hair naturally grows more slowly with age, and strands become finer and lighter. Nearly everyone has some hair loss with aging.

Female pattern hair loss vs telogen effluvium

Two kinds of hair loss are most common in midlife, and they behave differently:

Female pattern hair lossTelogen effluvium
What you noticeGradual thinning on the top and crown, often starting as a widening part; the front hairline usually staysDiffuse shedding all over the scalp, for example after childbirth
What’s happeningFollicles slowly shrink and make finer, shorter hairsMore hairs than usual move into the resting phase and fall out
CourseOngoing; MedlinePlus says untreated loss is permanent but rarely leads to near-total baldnessDepends on the trigger; see our telogen effluvium guide

The two can overlap. Read more in telogen effluvium and hair loss in women: causes and how to tell them apart.

The Kamp review also flags frontal fibrosing alopecia, a scarring condition linked to the perimenopausal years that causes the hairline at the front and temples to recede and can affect the eyebrows. Because it causes scarring, a receding front hairline is worth showing to a dermatologist promptly.

Medical causes worth ruling out

MedlinePlus says a clinician diagnosing female pattern hair loss checks for other causes, such as thyroid disease or iron deficiency, and for signs of too much androgen: unusual hair growth, period changes or new acne. The Kamp review adds that hormone tests are sometimes warranted when those signs are present, but that testing usually isn’t needed unless hair loss is severe or fast.

  • Thyroid: an underactive thyroid lists dry, thinning hair among its symptoms, and the US Office on Women’s Health says thyroid disease is more likely after menopause. See thyroid and hair loss.
  • Iron: MedlinePlus lists heavy menstrual bleeding among possible factors in female pattern hair loss, and iron deficiency among the causes to check. See iron, ferritin and hair loss.
  • Medicines: MedlinePlus names some medicines, including estrogen-containing birth control pills, among possible contributors. Don’t stop any medicine on your own; ask your prescriber.

What helps hair thinning in perimenopause?

OptionStatusWhat the sources say
Topical minoxidilFDA-approved, over the counterMedlinePlus says it may help hair grow in about 1 in 4 or 5 women and may slow or stop loss in most. The label says results may take 3 to 6 months, shedding can rise for up to 2 weeks at first, and regrown hair is likely lost within 3 to 4 months of stopping
Spironolactone (oral)Prescription; not FDA-approved for hair lossA 2023 meta-analysis of 5 studies (413 patients) found 56.6% improved overall, with higher rates when combined with minoxidil; the author called the evidence limited. See spironolactone for hair loss
Oral minoxidilPrescription; not FDA-approved for hair lossListed in the Kamp review as an option, with caution for people with heart conditions. See minoxidil for women
PRP and low-level laserIn-office or device-basedMedlinePlus lists both as options if minoxidil doesn’t work; the Kamp review says more research is needed on PRP outcomes
Hair transplantSurgeryMedlinePlus says results can be excellent for women who don’t respond to medicine, but it can take many sessions and carries some scarring and infection risk

Finasteride and dutasteride are also discussed in the Kamp review. They are prescription drugs not approved for women’s hair loss, and the review notes contraception is advised for premenopausal women taking dutasteride.

Minoxidil works only while you use it, and none of these options is a quick fix. MedlinePlus also notes that if you’re comfortable with your appearance, you don’t need treatment, and that styling changes and hairpieces are often the safest and least expensive route.

Does HRT help with menopausal hair thinning?

Not on current evidence. The Kamp review says there is insufficient evidence for hormone therapy as a treatment for female pattern hair loss, and that surprisingly little research has looked at hormone therapy and hair. Decisions about hormone therapy are about your overall health, made with your clinician, not about your hair alone.

Biotin and “hair vitamins”

NIH’s Office of Dietary Supplements says claims for biotin for hair rest, at best, on a few case reports and small studies, and a 2024 review found no studies showing benefit in healthy people. The Kamp review says more research is needed on supplements for hair. High-dose biotin can also cause falsely high or low results on some lab tests, including thyroid and heart tests, FDA warns. Details are in biotin for hair.

The honest bottom line

  • Thinning around menopause is common and is usually female pattern hair loss, sometimes mixed with shedding from another cause.
  • Get the cause checked, especially thyroid and iron, before spending on products.
  • See a dermatologist promptly for sudden or patchy loss, a receding front hairline, scalp pain, itching or redness.

Questions to ask a board-certified dermatologist:

  • Is this female pattern hair loss, shedding, or both?
  • Which blood tests make sense for me?
  • Is topical minoxidil a reasonable first step, and how long should I give it?
  • Are prescription options right for my health history?
