Zone 2 cardio for women: what it is and how much

“Zone 2” has become the cardio buzzword of the decade, sold as the best way to build your mitochondria and live longer. Here’s what it actually means, how much aerobic exercise official guidelines call for, and where the research is more modest than the marketing.

By the ÆTAS Research Desk · Checked against sources November 23, 2026 · How we work

Quick answer. Zone 2 is steady, easy-to-moderate cardio, roughly a pace where you can still talk but not sing. US guidelines call for 150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate activity (or 75 to 150 of vigorous). Steady cardio does build fitness and mitochondria, but reviews find no proof that zone 2 is the single best intensity; harder efforts and total amount matter too.
Review  Evidence at a glance
Best evidence
A large systematic review and meta-regression of exercise training and muscle mitochondria (353 studies, 5,650 participants in its main models); a 2025 narrative review of zone 2 in Sports Medicine; US Physical Activity Guidelines (2018)
Women included?
Yes, though the share isn’t clear. In the meta-regression, mitochondrial gains didn’t differ by sex. A 2025 lab study of how to define zone 2 included 20 women among 50 cyclists
Protocol tested
Continuous endurance training compared with interval and sprint training, over weeks; zone 2 itself has no single agreed definition
Bottom line
Steady cardio is a solid way to meet the guidelines. It’s not magic, and a mix of easy and harder work is also well supported

What is zone 2 cardio?

Exercise physiologists split effort into “zones” from very easy to all-out. Zone 2 is the second-lowest: steady work just below the point where your body starts relying much more on fast, sugar-burning energy and lactate begins to build up. Scientists call that point the first lactate or ventilatory threshold.

There’s no single agreed definition of zone 2. A 2025 lab study in Translational Sports Medicine tested 50 experienced cyclists (30 men and 20 women, average age about 31) and compared several ways of setting zone 2: fixed blood-lactate values, the first ventilatory threshold, the intensity of peak fat burning, a share of peak oxygen use, and a common rule of 72% to 82% of maximum heart rate.

  • The methods placed zone 2 at different intensities. On average, the 72% heart-rate cut-off and the point of peak fat burning sat well below the first ventilatory threshold.
  • On average, the 82% heart-rate cut-off landed at about the same power output as the ventilatory threshold.
  • Women’s heart rate at peak fat burning sat at a higher share of their maximum than men’s.

The authors concluded that fixed percentages of maximum heart rate are simple but may not reflect what’s happening in your muscles. The study was a one-time snapshot of fit cyclists on a bike, so it doesn’t tell us what training at each zone does over time.

How to find zone 2 without a lab: the talk test

Lab tests measure lactate in your blood or the gases you breathe out. At home, the simplest tool is one the CDC already uses for moderate intensity:

Talk test (CDC)You can talk, but not singYou can’t say more than a few words without pausing for breath
Effort on a 0–10 scale (CDC)5 or 6Starts at 7 or 8

These are general guides, not a precise match for a lab-measured threshold. The CDC also notes that what feels vigorous for one person can be moderate for a fitter one, which is why effort and breathing are useful checks. More on what wearables can and can’t measure lives in Health Trackers.

How much zone 2 cardio do women need?

No official guideline uses the term zone 2. The US Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans (2nd edition, 2018, from HHS) give the numbers that matter for adults:

  • Aerobic: 150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate activity, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, or a mix of the two.
  • Spread it out: preferably across the week.
  • Muscle-strengthening: moderate or harder work for all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week.
  • Some is better than none, and going beyond 300 minutes of moderate activity brings additional health benefits.

The guidelines count brisk walking at 2.5 to 4 mph as moderate. They also dropped an old rule that activity had to come in bouts of at least 10 minutes, so shorter sessions count.

Does zone 2 build mitochondria? What human studies show

Mitochondria are the parts of your cells that use oxygen to make energy. The main zone 2 claim is that steady, easy cardio is the best way to grow more of them.

The big picture from hundreds of studies

A large systematic review and meta-regression pooled training studies in human muscle published up to February 2022. Its main mitochondrial analyses covered 353 studies and 5,650 people, from young to over 55, untrained to well-trained, healthy and with conditions such as heart or metabolic disease. It compared continuous endurance training (low to moderate intensity), high-intensity interval training and sprint interval training.

Training typeAverage total training timeAdjusted rise in mitochondrial markers
Continuous endurance (low to moderate)About 39 hoursAbout 23%
High-intensity intervalsAbout 23 hoursAbout 27%
Sprint intervalsAbout 8 hoursAbout 27%
  • All three raised mitochondrial markers by similar amounts, but the harder types did it in less total time.
  • Bigger gains came with more volume and with higher intensity; the authors judged intensity multiplied by volume (training load) the best predictor.
  • People starting out less fit gained more.
  • The gains didn’t differ between women and men, or between younger and older people.

The review didn’t test “zone 2” by name, and it doesn’t say what share of participants were women.

A review that took on the zone 2 hype

A 2025 narrative review in Sports Medicine, titled “Much Ado About Zone 2,” looked at the case for zone 2 in the general public. Its authors found that the popular advice comes largely from observations of elite endurance athletes, who train for many hours a week and already have very high mitochondrial capacity. They concluded that current evidence doesn’t support zone 2 as the best intensity for building mitochondria or fat-burning capacity, and that working above zone 2 matters for heart and metabolic health, especially for people with limited time to train.

A narrative review is one group’s reading of the evidence, not a pooled analysis, so it’s best read alongside the larger review above. Both point the same way: steady cardio works, but it isn’t uniquely powerful.

What’s overclaimed about zone 2

  • “It’s the best intensity for mitochondria.” Not shown. Intervals raised mitochondrial markers as much or more in less time.
  • “Your watch knows your zone 2.” Fixed heart-rate percentages may not match your own threshold, and in the cyclist study the methods disagreed from person to person.
  • “Zone 2 is a longevity treatment.” Fitness is linked to lower death rates in observational studies (see VO2 max for women), but no trial shows that zone 2 in particular lengthens life.

What zone 2 does have going for it: it’s easy to recover from, easy to talk through, and a practical way to build up the minutes the guidelines call for.

What this means for you

  • Use the guidelines as your target. 150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate activity, or 75 to 150 vigorous, or a mix.
  • Use the talk test. If you can talk but not sing, you’re in moderate territory, close to what most people call zone 2.
  • Easy and hard both count. Steady cardio builds fitness; harder intervals are another well-studied option if you have less time. Our VO2 max guide covers interval trials in midlife women.
  • Keep lifting. Cardio doesn’t replace strength training for bone and muscle around menopause.
  • Fit it in. Brisk walks, cycling on flat ground and water aerobics all count, and short sessions add up.

If you haven’t been active, the guidelines advise starting low and going slow. If you have a heart condition, another health condition, an injury or symptoms, or haven’t been active, check with your clinician before starting.
